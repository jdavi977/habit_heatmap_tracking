# Goal System Research

## Well-known structured plans

1. **Benjamin Franklin** - Listed 13 virtues such as temperance, order, etc, and focused on one per week using a chart where he marked each day's lapses. His daily schedule started with the question *"What good shall I do this day?"* [1]

2. **Shohei Ohtani** - Baseball star that used a 9x9 grid where he wrote down a central goal and broke the goal into 8 supporting areas, he would then list eight concrete actions under each one. [2][3]

3. **Arnold Schwarzenegger** - Wrote down his goals and worked backwards from them. [6]

4. **Michael Phelps** - built training cycles backward from target race times, he would also rehearse these races mentally in detail. [7]

Despite these routines being different, they are built from the same foundation.

## How these methods actually work (detailed AI summary)

### Franklin’s virtue chart

He worked on one virtue each week, so a full pass through all 13 took 13 weeks, and he repeated the cycle four times a year. He kept a small book of charts with a column for each day and a row for each virtue, and every evening he reviewed his day and put a dot wherever he had slipped. He was honest about the results: he found far more faults than he expected, but he also got to watch them decrease. Over time he did the full course once a year, then once every several years, and eventually dropped it because of travel and business. [1]

### The Harada Method (Ohtani)

It was created by Takashi Harada, a junior high track coach who wanted to inspire underperforming athletes through discipline and self-reliance. The famous grid is only one of four tools. The others are a long-term goal form, a daily routine check sheet, and a daily diary. The guidance is to keep the daily checklist to 10 tasks or fewer, because the routine is supposed to make progress easier, not stall it from the start. [4] Ohtani’s grid produced 64 habits in total, and he set out to review it every day so no area fell behind. Interestingly, Harvard Business School professor Frances Frei found the inclusion of “karma” (character and luck) one of the most compelling parts of his chart. [2][3] The method is spreading: Oregon’s football team adopted it in 2026, with one ultimate goal, eight supporting pillars, and actions under each pillar. [5]

### Phelps and Bowman

Bowman’s approach is to start from a huge goal and work backward to simple steps you can take today. He and Phelps called this “Dream, plan, reach.” Phelps would write down his goal time plus three very specific things he had to do in practice to get there. Bowman separates long-term from short-term goals: dream goals can be emotional and vague, but short-term goals, such as a time in a specific event at a specific meet, should be detailed and objective. [7]

> **Complete operating systems:** target → breakdown → daily routine → recording → review.

## Important concepts to include

1. **Specific, hard goals beat vague ones.** Edwin Locke and Gary Latham found that specific goals outperform vague ones, and that harder goals produce more effort, focus, and persistence which lead to better performance. [8]

2. **Breaking goals into "when and how" actions.** These are similar to what psychologists call implementation intentions: specific plans for when, where, and how you'll act. Works best when the underlying commitment to the goal is already strong. [9][10]

3. **Recording progress, especially where others can see it.** One of the strongest concepts. A 2016 meta-analysis found that monitoring progress had larger effects on goal attainment when results were reported or made public, and when progress was physically recorded. [11]

4. **Writing it down and being accountable.** People who wrote their goals down achieved more than those who didn't, and people who sent weekly progress reports to a friend achieved the most. [12] *(Small study, 149 participants, not peer reviewed. Treat as suggestive.)*

5. **Visualizing obstacles, not just success.** Phelps's visualization not only looked for positive success but mentally prepared for anything that could go wrong. [7] The pleasure of positive fantasies lets us experience the wish virtually, but drains energy for the real work. [13] Instead we should pair both the dream with the obstacles for better preparation. [14]

6. **Routines that become automatic.** Every complete system runs on daily repetition, and habits take longer to form than people expect. The median time it took for a behavior to become close to automatic was 66 days (range 18 to 254 days). Missing one opportunity didn't change that. People who were very inconsistent didn't form habits at all. [15]

7. **Expecting to fall short.** No one can hit 100% of their goals. Systems succeed because partial progress toward a stretch goal still lands far ahead of where you'd be otherwise.

## Recipe combining popular systems

1. Pick one ambitious, specific target.
2. Break it into a handful of supporting areas, each with concrete when/where actions.
3. Identify the main obstacles in advance and plan your response to each.
4. Keep a short daily routine of no more than about 10 items, and record it on paper or in an app every day.
5. Send a progress report to one person each week.
6. Review and adjust on a fixed cycle (weekly or quarterly), and count roughly 70% of goal achieving as a win. [16] *(The 70% mark is the OKR convention, not a research finding.)*

## What to do when you have many ambitious goals?

1. **List and classify.** First list out your ambitious goals and figure out what type of goal it is.
    - **Practice goals** (get better through regular, repeated training)
    - **System goals** (few good decisions, set up once, do most of the work, and then maintain/adjust)
    - **Character goals** (about who you are and how you behave, never finish, progress shows up in moments and choices)
    - **Deadline goals** (fixed date and a clear finish line, more like projects than a lifestyle)

2. **Pick a lead goal and put the others on "maintenance".** Choose one goal to get your best energy this season (3-6 months). The others still get attention but in a smaller fixed amount (see step 5). Rotating a lead goal can come later (see step 7).

3. **Make each goal concrete.**

4. **Create a recipe for the lead goal.** We can use the Harada "Open Window 64 chart", this has three layers: [4]
    1. **Center square:** your one main target
    2. **Eight squares around it:** the eight support (pillars) needed to reach that target
    3. **Eight actions for each pillar:** each pillar becomes the center of its own small 3x3 block, surrounded by eight concrete actions. Giving 8x8 = 64 actions.

    In this case of multiple goals, only the lead goal gets the full grid, with its specific target in the center. A life vision that covers all the goals can sit as one sentence above the grid, but not in the center square, because it is too vague to break into concrete actions.

    Most of the 64 actions are not daily, some are weekly, and others monthly.

5. **Place the maintenance goals.** Every other goal we care about stays, whether or not it relates to the lead goal. Overlap only decides where it lives:
    - **Supports the lead goal:** it becomes one of the eight pillars inside the grid. Example: if the lead goal is a race time, sleep and strength are pillars.
    - **Unrelated to the lead goal:** it gets its own small card outside the grid, with one specific target and 1-3 minimum actions. Example: "save $500 a month, checked on the 1st" alongside that race goal.

    The real limit on maintenance goals is time and energy. Each one should be cheap enough that it doesn't take effort from the lead goal. If a goal can't be held at a minimum level right now, park it until a later season.

6. **Look for overlap and conflict.** See how these goals overlap with each other in a good or even conflicting way. Name tensions and decide how to handle it. If a goal actively conflicts with the lead goal, shrink its minimum or park it.

7. **Rotate.** When the lead goal changes, draw a new grid for the new lead goal and the old one drops to a maintenance card.

## How can we use AI to help?

1. **Pick and sharpen the target.** Give AI a fuzzy goal such as "be financially free" and ask it to push you with questions until the goal is concrete. Use AI to help classify what type of goal it is and to see if this goal is unrealistic.

2. **Break it down (64 grid).** AI can help draft a first version of the grid recipe including pillars and actions for each goal. Make sure to only use it as a starting point and edit it yourself.

3. **Plan for obstacles.** AI can be used to help imagine the obstacles that may come and give reasons why. It's good for spotting obstacles that you would rather not think about.

4. **Daily routine and recording.** AI can be used to spot patterns that you miss.

5. **Review and adjust.** AI can compare your actual progress against the targets and check if you meet the 70% goal mark. It can suggest what to change: such as dropping an action, adding one, or even rotating the lead goal.
