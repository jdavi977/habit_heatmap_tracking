Well-known structured plans include:

1. Benjamin Franklin - Listed 13 virtues such as temperance, order, etc, and focused on one per week using a chart where he marked each day's lapses. His daily schedule started with the question "What good shall I do this day?"

2. Shohei Ohtani - Baseball star that used a 9x9 grid where he wrote down a central goal and broke the goal into 8 supporting areas, he would then list eight concrete actions under each one.

3. Arnold Schwarzeneggar - Wrote down hit goals and worked backwards from them.

4. Michael Phelps - built training cycles backward from target race times, he would also rehearse these races mentally in detail.

Despite these routines being different, they are build from the same foundation.

Detailed AI summary of how these methods actually work:

Franklin’s virtue chart. He worked on one virtue each week, so a full pass through all 13 took 13 weeks, and he repeated the cycle four times a year. He kept a small book of charts with a column for each day and a row for each virtue, and every evening he reviewed his day and put a dot wherever he had slipped. He was honest about the results: he found far more faults than he expected, but he also got to watch them decrease. Over time he did the full course once a year, then once every several years, and eventually dropped it because of travel and business.

The Harada Method (Ohtani). It was created by Takashi Harada, a junior high track coach who wanted to inspire underperforming athletes through discipline and self-reliance. The famous grid is only one of four tools. The others are a long-term goal form, a daily routine check sheet, and a daily diary. The guidance is to keep the daily checklist to 10 tasks or fewer, because the routine is supposed to make progress easier, not stall it from the start. Ohtani’s grid produced 64 habits in total, and he set out to review it every day so no area fell behind. Interestingly, Harvard Business School professor Frances Frei found the inclusion of “karma” (character and luck) one of the most compelling parts of his chart. The method is spreading: Oregon’s football team adopted it this year, with one ultimate goal, eight supporting pillars, and actions under each pillar.

Phelps and Bowman. Bowman’s approach is to start from a huge goal and work backward to simple steps you can take today. He and Phelps called this “Dream, plan, reach.” Phelps would write down his goal time plus three very specific things he had to do in practice to get there. Bowman separates long-term from short-term goals: dream goals can be emotional and vague, but short-term goals, such as a time in a specific event at a specific meet, should be detailed and objective.
